About the zoo

Updated October 14, 2026

Digital Live Zoo gathers the free live animal cams on YouTube into one place you can browse like a zoo map. Hundreds of zoos, aquariums, sanctuaries, wildlife lodges, researchers and backyard naturalists stream around the clock, but their cams are scattered across channels and hard to find when something is actually happening. We find them, check every day that they are still live, sort them by continent and habitat, and keep a Main Stage running that tours the planet for you.

How cams get into the zoo

Every day we search YouTube for live streams of animals in a dozen languages, from “waterhole live cam” to “Storchennest live” and “動物園 ライブカメラ”. A cam is added only if it is a real live stream, shows animals, lets other sites embed it, and can be placed at least to a country. We leave out city, beach and weather cams, relaxing-music loops, “TV for cats” channels, reruns of documentaries, and anything marked as made for kids. When an owner restarts a stream, the cam keeps its place and its page; when a stream ends for good, it leaves the zoo.

How the Main Stage chooses what to show

Many cams spend long stretches showing an empty nest, a dark enclosure or a quiet waterhole. So the Main Stage does not just rotate through the most popular streams. Several times a day we look at the latest still frame of every cam and score it:

  • Is there something to look at? A small image model compares the frame with descriptions like “a bird sitting on its nest”, “eggs in a nest” or “an animal sleeping”, and with their opposites, such as “an empty nest” or “a dark night camera image”. It also compares each cam with its own usual picture, so a stork landing on a nest that is normally empty stands out.
  • Is anything moving? We compare the frame with the one before and with the cam’s usual background.
  • Are people watching? We read how many people are watching each stream right now, and flag a cam as trending when that number jumps well above its usual audience. A sudden crowd usually means something is going on: a hatching, a bear catching salmon, elephants arriving at the water.

The stage skips cams that show nothing or are off the air, stays longer on trending cams and cams with action, and falls back to the most-watched daytime cams when it has nothing better. The same score drives the “Most interesting now” sort and the badges on the cards.

Tips for watching

  • Follow the daylight. Most animals are easiest to see in the first and last hours of daylight. Turn on “Awake now” to see only cams where it is between 5 am and 8 pm, and check the local time on each card.
  • Nests run on seasons. Bald eagles in North America lay eggs in late winter, white storks in Europe return in spring, and ospreys raise chicks through early summer. Outside the season a nest cam may sit empty for months, then fill up within days.
  • Waterholes are busiest when it is dry. In southern Africa that is roughly May to October, when animals have few other places to drink. Mornings and late afternoons bring the most visitors.
  • Night cams use infrared. A grey, grainy picture is normal after dark. Owls, hedgehogs, badgers and many African animals are most active then.
  • Build a wall. Add up to nine cams to your wall to watch several places at once, for example every Brooks Falls angle during the salmon run.

Who owns the video

Every stream belongs to the zoo, park, organization or person who runs it, and plays from their own channel through YouTube’s player, so their views, chat and donations stay with them. Digital Live Zoo is an independent project and is not affiliated with any of them. If you run a cam and want it added, updated or removed, get in touch through the contact page.

Ads and statistics

The zoo is free. It may show a single ad at the top of the page, never in or over a video. We count visits anonymously to learn which features people use. The privacy page has the details.
